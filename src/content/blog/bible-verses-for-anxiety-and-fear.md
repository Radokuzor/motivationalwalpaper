Anxiety doesn't wait for a convenient time. It shows up at 2am, in the
parking lot before a hard conversation, in the space between sending a
message and getting a reply. If you're in a season like that, you don't
need a lecture about having more faith — you need something steady to hold
onto while the feeling passes. These verses are a starting point, with a
little context on what each one is actually saying, not just the reference.

## "Do not be anxious about anything" — Philippians 4:6-7

> "Do not be anxious about anything, but in every situation, by prayer and
> petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace
> of God, which transcends all understanding, will guard your hearts and
> your minds in Christ Jesus."

This is often quoted as a command to just stop worrying, which can feel
impossible when you're in it. Read closer, and it's not a command to feel
different — it's an instruction on what to *do* with the anxiety: bring it,
specifically, in prayer, rather than holding it alone. The peace described
isn't the absence of the hard situation. It's a peace that "transcends
understanding" — meaning it doesn't require the circumstances to make sense
first.

## "Cast all your anxiety on him" — 1 Peter 5:7

> "Cast all your anxiety on him because he cares for you."

The word "cast" implies throwing something away from yourself — not
managing it quietly, not being strong enough to carry it alone, but handing
it off entirely. This verse pairs the instruction with the reason: not
because you're supposed to be able to let go of things, but because you're
cared for enough that you don't have to carry it by yourself.

## "Fear not, for I am with you" — Isaiah 41:10

> "So do not fear, for I am with you; do not be dismayed, for I am your God.
> I will strengthen you and help you; I will uphold you with my righteous
> right hand."

This verse doesn't promise the fearful situation will disappear. It promises
presence and strength *inside* it. "Fear not" isn't a denial of the danger —
it's a reason not to face it alone.

## "God has not given us a spirit of fear" — 2 Timothy 1:7

> "For God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and
> of a sound mind."

If you've ever felt ashamed of being afraid, like it says something bad
about your faith, this verse pushes back directly. Fear isn't described here
as something God handed you to test you — it's described as something you
were never meant to be stuck with, because you were given power, love, and a
sound mind instead.

## "Be still, and know that I am God" — Psalm 46:10

> "Be still, and know that I am God; I will be exalted among the nations, I
> will be exalted in the earth."

Anxiety often demands motion — fix it, solve it, figure it out right now.
This verse asks for the opposite: stillness first, understanding second. It
suggests that some peace is only available once you stop trying to control
the outcome for a moment.

## "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want" — Psalm 23:1

> "The Lord is my shepherd, I lack nothing. He makes me lie down in green
> pastures, he leads me beside quiet waters, he refreshes my soul."

Psalm 23 is often read at funerals, but it was written as a description of
daily provision and care, not just a verse for grief. "He makes me lie down"
is worth sitting with — rest here isn't something you have to fight for on
your own; it's something you're led into.

## "Peace I leave with you" — John 14:27

> "Peace I leave with you; my peace I give you. I do not give to you as the
> world gives. Do not let your hearts be troubled and do not be afraid."

The phrase "not as the world gives" matters. Worldly peace usually depends
on circumstances lining up — good news, resolved conflict, a clear path
forward. This peace is offered as something separate from those conditions,
which is exactly the kind of peace anxiety can't reach with logic alone.

## How to actually use these verses in an anxious moment

Reading a list of verses once won't change much on its own. What tends to
help is picking one — just one — that genuinely resonates, and returning to
it deliberately: written on a card in your bag, said out loud during a
panic spiral, the first thing you read in the morning during a hard season.
Repetition is what turns a verse from something you know into something
that's actually there when you need it.
